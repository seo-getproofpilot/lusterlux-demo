How to clean a golf cart

Carts get treated like garden furniture and priced like motorcycles. Here is how to keep one looking like the second thing.

The short version

  1. Clear the cart out — floor mats, bag, cupholders, everything.
  2. Clean painted panels and plastic bodywork one section at a time.
  3. Wipe seats and dash with an interior cleaner that dries clean.
  4. Scrub the wheels, then dress the tires last.
  5. Wipe the windshield with a waffle-weave towel, inside and out.

Why carts get neglected

A cart lives outside, gets driven through wet grass and sand, and almost never sees a hose — because most of them are parked somewhere without a spigot, and because nobody thinks of a cart as a vehicle you detail.

Meanwhile a decent cart costs as much as a used car and holds its value about as well. The neglect is a habit, not a decision.

Start by emptying it

Floor mats out, bag out, cupholders out, everything from under the seat. Carts collect an extraordinary amount of grass clippings and sand in places you cannot see until the seat is up.

Shake the mats out and hose them if you can. Blow or brush out the footwells before any liquid goes near them — wet grass clippings smear, dry ones sweep.

The bodywork

Cart bodies are almost entirely painted panels and moulded plastic, and they take exactly the same cart-path dust and pollen a car does, with none of the wash access.

BirdieLux is built for this: a waterless cleaner formulated for cart paint, plastic body panels and trim that lifts grime without streaking, so a cart can be cleaned where it sits. Work one panel at a time, mist an even coat, wipe with a clean edgeless microfiber, then buff with a dry side.

Same rules as any waterless wash apply. If the panel feels gritty rather than dusty, rinse it first. If you are out of clean towel faces, stop.

The windshield deserves its own paragraph

This is where carts get ruined.

Cart windshields are usually acrylic or polycarbonate. They scratch at a fraction of the force glass does, and they craze — develop a network of fine cracks — on contact with ammonia, which is in most household glass cleaners.

So: never dry-wipe it, never use blue window cleaner, and never use a paper towel. Flood the surface with product, wipe with a clean waffle-weave towel, and flip to a dry face to finish. If it is hinged, do both sides.

Seats and dash

Cart seats live outdoors and absorb sunscreen, sweat and rain all season. They also have no seatbelts, which makes one property matter more than it does in a car: whatever you clean them with cannot leave them slick.

XFresh cleans cart seats, dash and trim and dries clean, without the greasy film that turns a bench seat into a slide. Spray onto the towel rather than the seat, work a section at a time, and follow with a dry side. A curved detail brush gets into the seams and textured vinyl where a towel just skates over the top.

Wheels and tires

Carts have brakes, so they have brake dust, just less of it. LuxWheelAssassin breaks it down chemically so you are not scrubbing a painted or clear-coated cart wheel, and it is safe on the clear-coated, painted, chrome and alloy finishes carts actually use. Always test an inconspicuous area first.

Dress the tires last, after everything else is done and the wheel is dry. TireVenom dries to the touch, which on a cart matters mostly because the tires are inches from the seats people are about to sit on.

Keep the battery bay dry

Whatever else you do, keep liquid away from the battery compartment, the controller and the wiring loom. Wipe those areas with a barely damp towel or leave them alone. A cart that will not start because you flooded a connector is an expensive clean.

Do it in fifteen minutes, often

The honest advice is that carts do not need detailing. They need wiping down.

Fifteen minutes every couple of weeks — panels, seats, windshield — and a cart never accumulates the baked-on film that requires a real session. The FairWay Finish System bundles the cleaners, brushes and microfiber for exactly that routine, which is mostly a way of making sure the products live *with* the cart rather than in a garage across town.

The seasonal reset

Twice a year, do the version that takes an hour rather than fifteen minutes.

Seats out or folded fully forward, floor mats out and washed, under-seat storage emptied and vacuumed. Clean and dress every plastic panel including the ones behind the seats that nobody sees. Deep clean the wheels, dress the tires, and go over the windshield properly on both sides.

This is also when you look at the things that are not cleaning: tire pressures, battery terminals for corrosion, water levels if it is a lead-acid pack, and whether the charge port is clean and dry. A cart that gets looked at twice a year does not develop expensive surprises.

Sand is the enemy, not dirt

Cart-path sand is angular and it gets everywhere — into seams, under seat cushions, into the floor texture, and into your towel.

That last one is the problem. Sand in a microfiber towel turns it into fine-grit sandpaper, and cart bodywork is generally softer than automotive clear coat. Shake towels out often, rotate faces aggressively, and when in doubt use a fresh one. Blowing or brushing the loose sand off *before* anything wet touches the cart is the single biggest thing you can do to avoid marring.

Storage and covers

A cart left uncovered outdoors ages roughly as fast as a convertible with the top down. Sun destroys seat vinyl first, then the plastic bodywork, then anything rubber.

If it lives outside, cover it — and put the cover on a clean, dry cart, because a cover over a dusty cart traps grit against the panels and the cover itself becomes the abrasive. If covering is impractical, keeping the vinyl and plastics protected with RestorX does real work; it holds up to six months on exterior surfaces.

Lifted and off-road carts

Carts with real tires and lift kits get treated more like a UTV, which means mud rather than dust, and mud needs a different approach. Let it dry, knock the bulk off, then foam and rinse rather than trying to wipe it.

Everything about the interior and windshield stays the same — those are still soft materials that scratch, and being covered in trail dust does not make them tougher.

Questions.

Can I pressure wash a golf cart?
Be careful. Carts have exposed wiring, a battery bay, a controller and connectors that were never designed for a direct high-pressure jet. If you use a pressure washer at all, keep it low, keep it wide, and stay well away from the battery compartment and any loom. Waterless or hand washing avoids the question entirely.
What can I use on a cart windshield?
Most cart windshields are acrylic or polycarbonate, not glass, and they scratch far more easily. Never dry-wipe one. Flood it with product, wipe with a clean waffle-weave towel, and never use an ammonia-based glass cleaner — ammonia crazes acrylic.
How often should a cart be cleaned?
More often than most people do, and less thoroughly each time. A cart that gets wiped down every couple of weeks never needs a real detail. One that gets ignored for a season needs an afternoon.
