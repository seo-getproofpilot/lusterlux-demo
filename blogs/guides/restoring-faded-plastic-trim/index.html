Restoring faded plastic trim: what works and what doesn't

Grey, chalky trim is what makes a clean car still look tired. Most of the fixes people try are temporary, and one of them is genuinely bad advice.

The short version

  1. Clean the trim thoroughly and let it dry — dressing over dirt locks it in.
  2. Apply a thin coat of dressing with an applicator pad.
  3. Work it into the texture rather than laying it on top.
  4. Let it level for a minute.
  5. Wipe back any excess with a dry towel so nothing stays tacky.

What fading actually is

Exterior trim is usually textured polypropylene, and it fails from the outside in. UV cracks the polymer chains at the surface while the plasticisers that keep it flexible slowly migrate out and wash away.

What you are left with is a microscopically rough, porous, oxidised layer. A smooth black surface reflects light. A rough one scatters it. That scattering is the grey.

This matters because it tells you what a fix has to do: fill and seal that porous layer, and block the UV that caused it. Anything that only adds temporary shine is treating the symptom.

What does not work

Heat guns. They do work, briefly — heat brings residual oils to the surface and the trim darkens. But you have added no UV protection, so it fades again, and meanwhile you are holding a 500-degree tool against a plastic panel with clips behind it. Warping, scorching and melted mounting tabs are common outcomes. High risk, temporary result.

Peanut butter, olive oil, and the rest of the internet. Oils darken porous plastic for a few days, then go rancid, attract dust, and wash off in the first rain. They are also food, which is exactly as good an idea outdoors as it sounds.

Interior protectants used outside. They are formulated for a cabin — low UV load, no rain, no road salt. Outside they last a week.

Anything greasy. A permanently tacky surface parked outdoors collects every bit of dust in the air. Two weeks later the trim looks worse than when it was faded, and now it is faded *and* dirty.

What does work

Clean first, then dress with something that levels into the surface and carries real UV protection.

Cleaning is not optional. Dressing over dirt seals it in and the trim comes out blotchy. Use a dedicated cleaner — InteriorX handles plastics and rubber and dries without residue — and get into the texture with a brush if the trim is heavily grained. Let it dry completely.

Then dress thin. RestorX is built for rubber, vinyl and plastic across trim, dashboards, engine bay components and tires. Apply a thin coat with an applicator pad, work it into the texture rather than laying it on top, let it level for a minute, then wipe back anything that has not absorbed.

That last step is what separates a factory-looking finish from a shiny plastic look. Trim is not supposed to gloss. It is supposed to be evenly, deeply black.

Interior plastics are a different problem

Inside the cabin, UV is lower but the failure mode is worse: anything that leaves shine puts glare in your windshield, and anything that leaves slickness on a steering wheel is a safety issue rather than an aesthetic one.

Clean interior plastics to a factory matte and leave them there. If a dash is genuinely faded rather than dirty, a thin coat of RestorX levelled back hard will even it out — RestorX holds up to eight months inside, against six months on exterior trim, because it is not fighting the same UV load.

Realistic expectations

Restoration brings back colour and blocks further degradation. It does not rebuild polymer that has already broken down. On trim that has gone truly chalky and rough, expect a large improvement rather than a factory-new finish, and expect the first application to soak in fast — porous plastic drinks product. A second thin coat after the first has levelled is normal.

Once it is back, keeping it there is easy. Reapply when it starts looking dry, roughly twice a year on a car that lives outside.

Test somewhere invisible first

Trim varies enormously — textured polypropylene, smooth ABS, rubber seals, and painted plastic that only looks like trim. They do not all respond identically.

Pick somewhere that does not show. The underside of a mirror housing, the lower edge of a rear bumper, the inside of a fuel door. Clean it, dress it, and look at it in daylight before you commit to the whole car. Two minutes there beats discovering that a panel you thought was textured plastic is actually satin-painted.

Engine bay plastics

The engine bay is where trim restoration pays off most visibly and where almost nobody bothers.

Work on a cool engine. Cover the alternator, any exposed intake and the fuse box. Clean the plastic covers and ducting, let everything dry fully, then apply a thin coat of RestorX and level it back. Faded black plastic covers going back to even black transforms how a bay reads — far more than polishing anything metal does.

Nothing here should end up wet or tacky. If it looks glossy, you have used too much.

Rubber seals need the same attention

Door and window seals are rubber, they take the same UV, and when they dry out they crack, whistle at speed, and eventually leak. They are also the reason a door sounds cheap when it closes.

Same treatment: clean, dry, thin coat, level it back. On seals, the goal is suppleness rather than appearance, so wipe back harder than you would on visible trim — you do not want product transferring onto the door edge or your clothes.

Keeping it, rather than redoing it

The reason trim fades in the first place is unprotected UV exposure, so the maintenance question is simply whether there is a protective layer on it or not.

Twice a year on a car that lives outside, once a year on a garaged one. If you can see it starting to go dry and flat at the edges, that is the signal — not a date. Reapplying to trim that is still protected takes ten minutes. Restoring trim that has been left for three years takes an afternoon and never comes back quite as well.

Questions.

Why does trim fade in the first place?
UV breaks down the polymer at the surface and the plasticisers that keep it flexible migrate out. What is left is a rough, porous, oxidised layer that scatters light instead of reflecting it — which is what you read as grey and chalky.
Does a heat gun really work?
It can bring colour back temporarily by bringing remaining oils to the surface, and it can also warp the panel, scorch it, or damage the clip mounts behind it. It does not add UV protection, so the fade returns. It is a lot of risk for a result a dressing achieves safely.
How long should a trim restorer last?
RestorX holds up to six months on exterior trim and up to eight months on interior surfaces, depending on sun exposure and how often the vehicle is washed.
